Reviewers who work with an AI assistant look completely fine — until the AI is taken away

Every reviewer here spent 24 weeks judging cases with AI help. If you only look at normal metrics — accuracy, throughput, how often they agreed with the AI — every arm of the study looks equally healthy, 84–87% accurate the whole way through. Nothing looks wrong.

Then, in week 25, the AI was taken away and everyone was tested on their own. The real picture — real skill, quietly built up or eroded by how each reviewer used the AI for those 24 weeks — was hiding underneath the whole time:

How reviewers used the AILooked like, week 1–24Really was, week 25 (AI removed)
Always shown the AI's answer86.6% accurate60.9% accurate
AI hidden on some cases84.1% accurate69.7% accurate
Prompted to double-check the AI87.3% accurate80.0% accurate
Had to judge before seeing the AI87.1% accurate81.7% accurate
Same starting accuracy, wildly different real skill six months later — and the first column gave no warning. Everything below is this dashboard's attempt to recover that hidden second column without ever taking the AI away, using only the kind of log any real deployment already collects.

Does the difficulty proxy generalize to real medical images?

external validation · A.4-stretch
D4's cost/benefit numbers rely on the A.4 difficulty proxy — the idea that low AI confidence signals a harder case. That was built and validated on the synthetic dataset's single binary AI decision per case. Separately, the same idea was tested on 300 real, labeled NIH ChestX-ray14 images, scored by a frozen, pre-existing public model (nothing was trained here) — a genuinely different, multi-label task.

Why it inverts — the model used (torchxrayvision's densenet121-res224-nih, frozen, nothing trained here) almost always fires on something:

Calling "abnormal" on 93.8% of genuinely normal images means the binary triage accuracy on this sample is 48.3% — chance. "Confidence" here is max(probability) across 14 disease channels: one huge, unambiguous finding can dominate that max even when the overall case has several marginal comorbid findings alongside it — a reduction artifact from collapsing a multi-label problem into one number, not a failure of the underlying model.
Honest read: the narrow claim — a low-confidence prediction is more likely a wrong one — survives the move to real data. The broader claim the proxy was built for — that confidence-based clustering recovers case complexity — does not, and inverts with strong significance (p=2×10⁻¹³). This doesn't invalidate the synthetic-dataset validation D4 relies on; it means the method shouldn't be assumed to carry over unmodified to a genuinely multi-label deployment without redesigning how "confidence" is computed. Full writeup, tier-proportion comparison, and methodology: A4_STRETCH_CHESTXRAY14.md.

Does this actually work outside the dataset?

live experiment · B.4
Everything above — including the cost/benefit trade in D4 — is a simulation of what the B.2 intervention policy would do, run against a synthetic log. Separately, the actual hypothesis behind it (periodic unassisted practice preserves more real skill than constant AI assistance) was tested on 59 real people, split into two groups, across three independently run task variants. Group A saw AI help on every training item; Group B had it periodically withheld — the same idea as the B.2 policy. Both groups were then tested completely unassisted.

Worth naming plainly: every gk_study p-value dropped substantially as that source grew from 31 to 47 participants (0.825→0.554) — moving toward significance, not away, while the effect's direction held. That's consistent with a real, small, underpowered effect — but exactly as consistent with noise from a handful of correlated data batches. 59 isn't enough to tell those apart, and this shouldn't be read as "getting close."
Honest read: the periodic-blind group did better in every slice of the data (each task variant alone, pooled, with or without low-effort participants excluded) — the direction the intervention hypothesis predicts. But at n=59, none of it clears conventional statistical significance. This is not evidence the intervention works, and not evidence it doesn't — a real, honestly-reported null result, which the underlying research plan explicitly said should outweigh a simulated positive. Full protocol and data-quality handling (duplicates, chance-rate corrections, low-effort flagging): B4_LIVE_EXPERIMENT_REPORT.md.
